I believe that a balanced life requires us to pay attention to three parts of ourselves: the body, the mind and the spirit.

And between them is something remarkably simple:

**the breath.**

We breathe without thinking about it.

From our first breath to our last, breathing happens continuously. Yet our breath is also intimately connected with our emotional and physical state.

When we are frightened, our breathing often becomes fast and shallow.

When we are anxious, we may hold our breath without even noticing.

When we are deeply relaxed, our breathing naturally becomes slower and easier.

The breath seems to sit somewhere between the conscious and unconscious parts of ourselves.

That makes it a fascinating bridge between body and mind.

## The body

The body is where we physically experience our lives.

It needs movement, nourishment, rest, touch, sunlight, fresh air and sleep.

When we ignore the body for long enough, it eventually begins to communicate with us — through fatigue, tension, pain, illness or exhaustion.

Learning to listen to the body is therefore an important part of wellbeing.

## The mind

Our minds are constantly interpreting our experiences.

Thoughts influence emotions. Emotions influence behaviour. And our psychological state can also influence what happens physically in the body.

Modern psychology and medicine increasingly recognise the importance of the relationship between psychological and physical health. The American Psychological Association, for example, describes the mind–body connection in relation to conditions such as gastrointestinal health and integrated care. ([APA](https://www.apa.org/topics/health-care/gastrointestinal-health))

We cannot always control what happens to us.

But we can learn to become more aware of what is happening inside us — our thoughts, emotions, reactions and patterns.

Awareness gives us choice.

## The spirit

The word *spirit* can mean different things to different people.

It does not have to mean religion.

For one person, spirituality may be a relationship with God. For another, it may be nature, meditation, creativity, service, love, community or a sense of meaning and purpose.

For me, the spiritual part of ourselves is connected to the experience of being more than simply our thoughts and our physical body.

It is the part that asks:

> What gives my life meaning?
>
> What am I connected to?
>
> What do I want my life to stand for?

A healthy life needs more than physical survival. We need a reason to get up in the morning.

## And then there is the breath

The breath connects these worlds.

We can deliberately change our breathing, and research suggests that controlled breathing can influence both psychological and physiological states.

A systematic review of diaphragmatic breathing found evidence for reductions in physiological and psychological stress. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31436595/))

Another systematic review examined 19 studies of breathing interventions and found that many reported improvements in anxiety and stress, while also noting that larger, high-quality randomised trials are still needed. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39150318/))

A separate systematic review of 58 studies found that many breathing interventions were effective for reducing stress or anxiety, particularly when practices were undertaken repeatedly rather than as a single brief intervention. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38137060/))

Research has also looked at physical effects. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 studies found that breathing exercises were associated with modest reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure and heart rate. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38179185/))

So while we should not claim that breathing is a cure for disease, there is growing scientific evidence that the way we breathe can influence how we feel and how our bodies respond to stress.

And this makes intuitive sense.

When we consciously slow down our breathing, we are giving ourselves a moment to stop.

To notice.

To come back into the body.

To create a little space between what is happening and how we respond to it.

## Coming back to ourselves

Perhaps this is why simple practices such as conscious breathing, meditation, yoga, walking in nature and mindful movement can feel so powerful.

They bring our attention back into the present moment.

Instead of living entirely in our thoughts, we become aware of our body.

Instead of being completely carried away by our emotions, we notice them.

Instead of moving through life automatically, we pause.

We breathe.

And sometimes, in that pause, we remember ourselves.

## Balance rather than perfection

I don’t believe that a balanced life means being calm and happy all the time.

Life isn’t like that.

We experience grief, anger, fear, excitement, desire, disappointment and joy.

Balance doesn’t mean eliminating these experiences.

It means developing the capacity to move through them without losing ourselves completely.

The body needs care.

The mind needs attention.

The spirit needs meaning.

And the breath can help us move between them.

For me, this is the beginning of a truly holistic approach to wellbeing:

> Body. Mind. Spirit. Breath.

Not four separate things, but parts of one human experience.

When we learn to listen to all of them, we become better able to notice what we need — and to make the small, loving choices that help us live a more balanced life.
